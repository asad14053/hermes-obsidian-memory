# Bangladesh's cyber-law test: protection or another speech-control tool?

## TOPIC

Bangladesh's proposed 2026 cyber-law amendment: can it tackle deepfakes, fraud, sextortion, and online harassment without recreating the fear and discretion associated with earlier digital-security laws?

This wins because it joins an immediate public problem with a question viewers can use: how to tell a necessary cybercrime law from a vague speech-control law. It is timely without depending on outrage. The main evidence gap is equally important: the exact September draft reviewed by the government and TIB was not located in the public source set.

## CORE THESIS

Bangladesh needs an effective law against hacking, online fraud, sextortion, deepfakes, and targeted harassment. But a democratic cyber law cannot be judged by a minister's stated intention. It must be judged by its words and safeguards: Can an ordinary person predict what is illegal? Who authorizes a takedown, search, or arrest? Is there independent review? Does the affected person receive notice and a real appeal?

## VIEWER QUESTION

Can Bangladesh stop genuine online harm without handing the state a vague tool that can also be used against criticism, journalism, satire, or dissent?

## WHY NOW

The government held a media consultation on 10 September and said the draft was still being revised to remove ambiguous language and prevent misuse.[1] On 11 September, TIB argued that the draft still used broad terms, made one cited offence non-bailable, concentrated authority in a government-led council, and included "good faith" immunity.[2] That puts the story in the most useful window: before the final text is settled, when scrutiny can still change it.

The amendment process began with a stated aim of addressing rumours, misinformation, defamation, AI-generated deceptive media, and time-bound platform takedowns.[3] Those are real policy questions. The issue is whether one criminal-law framework can handle them without sweeping lawful speech into the same net.

## STORY STRUCTURE

1. Start with the harm, not the politics: a deepfake, sextortion threat, scam, or coordinated harassment can ruin a person's life quickly.
2. Give the government's strongest case: the state says the law is meant to address those harms and that ambiguous language is being revised.[1][3]
3. Put the disputed clauses on screen: TIB's concerns about vague definitions, bail, the council, and immunity.[2]
4. Test the draft against five safeguards: precision, separation of crimes from speech regulation, judicial authorization, notice and appeal, and public accountability.[4][5]
5. End with a conditional verdict: the law may be necessary, but viewers should not accept either "trust us" or "every cyber law is censorship." Demand the exact text and check the safeguards.

## KEY EVIDENCE

### Confirmed

- On 10 September, the Information and Broadcasting Minister said the government did not intend to suppress free thought, the press, or journalists. He also said the draft was being revised to avoid ambiguous words and that consultation with media professionals was under way.[1]
- The government's stated policy targets include rumours, misinformation, defamatory content, AI-generated deceptive media, and faster removal by international platforms.[3]
- TIB's 11 September statement says Section 46(2) treats Section 23 as non-bailable. It also describes a 28-member council with only two private-sector experts, both government-nominated, and a clause protecting empowered officials from civil and criminal proceedings for acts done in "good faith." These are TIB's readings of the draft, not independently checked quotations from the draft itself.[2]
- Bangladesh's Constitution guarantees freedom of thought and conscience, speech, expression, and the press, while permitting reasonable restrictions imposed by law for specified interests.[5]
- The ICT Division hosts an enacted "Cyber Protection Act, 2026" with a publication date of 15 April 2026.[7] The September sources use both "Cyber Security (Amendment) Act" and "Cyber Protection (Amendment) Act." Do not silently treat those titles as interchangeable until the current draft is obtained.

### Historical and policy context

- In March 2025, the UN Special Rapporteur on freedom of expression warned that putting technical cybercrime and online content in the same criminal-law framework creates different enforcement problems. The letter called for clear definitions and judicial authorization for content removal and blocking.[4]
- Amnesty's 2024 report argued that the 2023 Cyber Security Act continued a pattern of using speech offences against peaceful dissent and called for repeal or amendment to international standards.[6]
- The past does not prove that the 2026 draft will be abused. It explains why the burden of proof now sits with the text, oversight, and remedy, not with assurances alone.

### Internal inconsistency that needs checking

A participant quoted by BSS praised "the removal of non-bailable provisions," while TIB said Section 46(2) still makes Section 23 non-bailable.[1][2] These statements may refer to different offences or different draft versions. The video should show the discrepancy and refuse to resolve it without the dated draft.

## COUNTERARGUMENT / ALTERNATIVE VIEW

The government's case should be presented seriously. Fraud, sextortion, non-consensual sexual imagery, impersonation, and malicious deepfakes can cause immediate harm. Slow platform response and weak enforcement can leave victims exposed.[1][3] A video that treats every enforcement power as censorship will lose credibility.

The answer is not to abandon cyber law. It is to separate conduct that attacks systems from rules that regulate speech, define offences narrowly, require intent and demonstrable harm where appropriate, use civil remedies for ordinary defamation, put coercive action under independent judicial control, and guarantee notice, appeal, and transparency. The UN Special Rapporteur made the same basic distinction in the 2025 review of Bangladesh's earlier draft.[4]

### 5:35–7:55 | The five tests

1. Precision: Can an ordinary person know what conduct is prohibited?
2. Separation: Are hacking and infrastructure attacks kept distinct from criticism, defamation, or harmful content?
3. Independent authorization: Does a judge approve takedowns, blocking, search, seizure, or arrest?
4. Remedy: Does the affected user get notice, reasons, and an appeal?
5. Accountability: Are orders, statistics, errors, reversals, and agency conduct open to public scrutiny?

Article 39 protects speech and the press while allowing reasonable legal restrictions.[5] The international test asks whether restrictions are provided by clear law, pursue a legitimate aim, and are necessary and proportionate.[4]
