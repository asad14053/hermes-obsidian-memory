## CORE THESIS

ভিডিওর কাজ হবে তিনটি হিসাব আলাদা করা: কে অসুস্থ বা মারা গেছেন বলে সন্দেহভাজন, কার সংক্রমণ পরীক্ষায় নিশ্চিত, এবং কোন শিশুর টিকা পাওয়ার কথা থাকলেও বাস্তবে পৌঁছায়নি।[1][2][3]

## KEY EVIDENCE

### E1 — Current total, with categories kept separate

The October 31 update reports one additional child death with measles-like symptoms and a combined total of 1,252.[1] It separates 1,147 suspected measles-related deaths from 105 laboratory-confirmed deaths.[1]

Evidence class: current newsroom report quoting DGHS.[1] The underlying October 31 DGHS bulletin was not retrieved as a stable public file, so the brief must keep that attribution visible.

### E2 — Suspected and confirmed are different surveillance categories

The Bangladesh guideline definition reproduced in the CDC-hosted report identifies a suspected case from symptoms and clinical suspicion, while confirmation requires a positive measles test after excluding vaccine-associated symptoms.[2]

The same report says the large suspected-to-confirmed gap partly reflects routine surveillance practice, but may also reflect limited testing and unavailable case-level linkage.[2] It warns against treating the reported case-fatality rate as a population-wide rate.[2]

### E3 — The campaign was large, but the target population was not fully captured

WHO records a targeted campaign beginning April 5 and a nationwide campaign beginning April 20.[4] The October IFRC update says nearly 20 million children had been vaccinated through emergency and follow-up campaigns, yet a September 26 campaign still targeted about 3.4 million unvaccinated and missed children.[3]

These are programme reports, not proof that every reported dose reached a unique eligible child or that every target was counted correctly.[3][4]

### E4 — Administrative coverage can look better than population immunity

The CDC-hosted analysis reports that some administrative coverage figures exceeded 100% while using 2011 census denominators, which can mask denominator error and over-reporting.[2]

Reuters reports that WHO and UNICEF estimated 2025 first-dose coverage at 90% and second-dose coverage at 86%, below the 95% level WHO recommends for both doses.[4][5]

### E5 — Preparedness and outreach are part of the story

Reuters reports that the April campaign used one readiness-check round rather than the usual four because of time pressure, and that meeting minutes later described the share of unvaccinated children among confirmed cases as virtually unchanged before and after the campaign.[5]

This reporting supports an accountability question.[5] It does not establish that one official, one parent group or vaccine hesitancy alone caused the outbreak.[5]

## COUNTERARGUMENT / ALTERNATIVE VIEW

The response was not absent.[3][4]
WHO records a rapid targeted start and a nationwide launch in April, while IFRC reports nearly 20 million vaccinations across emergency and follow-up campaigns.[3][4]

A fair video should also explain why suspected cases outnumber confirmed cases: surveillance systems do not test every clinically suspected case, especially during a large outbreak.[2] The gap is not, by itself, evidence of fabricated deaths or failed vaccination.

The strongest alternative reading is that officials faced a fast-moving outbreak and expanded vaccination quickly, while incomplete denominators and hard-to-reach populations made “everyone covered” much harder than the headline target suggested.[2][3][5] The video should test that explanation with records, not dismiss it.

## WHAT MAKES MY VERSION DIFFERENT

The video does not choose between vaccine cheerleading and vaccine panic. It audits the counting system. The viewer sees why a campaign can report a huge number of doses and still miss children when the target list, denominator or outreach is incomplete.[2][3]

The visual payoff is a public scorecard, not another montage of hospital beds. The same standard applies to government claims and criticism: show the denominator, preserve the category labels and say which record is still missing.
