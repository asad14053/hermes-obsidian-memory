A Bangla explainer about what the New Mooring Container Terminal concession changes, what the published financial figures do and do not establish, and what evidence would let citizens judge the deal. Scope is NCT and its Overflow Container Yard, not the sale or transfer of the whole Chattogram Port.[1]

## CORE THESIS

CPA retains ownership while DP World receives operating and maintenance responsibility under a 15-year concession, according to the signing release.[1] That settles the ownership-versus-operation distinction, but it does not settle whether the deal is good value.

Our editorial test: compare enforceable payments, costs, service improvements and risks against a credible alternative operating plan. Do not treat an upfront payment as a sale price, a revenue share as profit, or a promise of efficiency as an achieved result. Public summaries alone do not support a confident net-gain or net-loss verdict; the reporting itself leaves the revenue formula and deductions unresolved.[2][4]

## STORY STRUCTURE

1. **What changed?** Establish the signed concession and distinguish the terminal's owner from its operator. Keep NCT visually separate from the whole port.[1]
2. **Follow one payment.** Separate the reported upfront payment, future equipment investment, annual fee and variable revenue share. They are not interchangeable amounts, and their payment status is not identical.[2][4]
3. **Ask what the percentage applies to.** Put the different revenue-share descriptions side by side. Explain the need for the actual tiers, denominator and allowed deductions before calculating returns.[2][4][7]
4. **Test the promises fairly.** Present the government's efficiency and worker-protection case alongside the workers' transparency and job-security concerns; neither is a measured outcome yet.[1][2][3]
5. **Leave with a usable test.** Propose a public scorecard: verified CPA receipts, independently measured operating performance, and enforceable worker/asset safeguards. Give no profit verdict until the missing schedules and baseline are available.

### Internal checks and source conflicts

- **Revenue share:** do not silently replace 67% with 40–67%, or call one outlet wrong. The descriptions could reflect tiers or different assumptions. Until the schedule is obtained, use an attributed side-by-side comparison and no exact 15-year revenue forecast.[2][4][7]
- **Throughput inconsistency:** TBS reports NCT handling 13.85 lakh TEUs, labels it roughly 44% of total port handling, and later gives total port handling as 3.52 million TEUs for FY26.[2] A Python check gives `1.385 / 3.52 × 100 = 39.3466%`, not 44%. This flags a denominator/period/value question, not a verified replacement statistic. Exclude the 44% graphic and the article's derived NCT revenue estimate until CPA records reconcile them.
- **Vintage mismatch:** the PPPA profile still says “development phase” and explicitly carries a January 11 update date.[10] The October 8 signing release is newer.[1] Use the profile for project history only; its old status is not evidence that the signing did not happen.
- **Package mismatch:** the older USD205 million feasibility estimate and the later roughly USD150 million investment-and-other-commitments package need not measure the same thing.[2][10] Do not label their difference a discount, saving, loss or disappearance of money.
- **Modality:** “will invest”, “expects”, “government says” and “workers announced” must remain future commitments or attributed positions. They are not outcomes. TBS presents six-month commencement and two-year full operations; Times distinguishes contractual handover allowance from a minister's shorter expectation.[2][7] Leave a precise takeover countdown out until the signed milestones are read.

## COUNTERARGUMENT / ALTERNATIVE VIEW

The strongest pro-deal argument deserves a fair presentation: ownership need not change for an experienced operator to improve equipment use, maintenance and logistics. CPA and DP World say the arrangement aims to improve operations and connectivity; the government also presents investment and worker safeguards as benefits.[1][2]

Workers' concerns are about transparency, jobs, control and whether the investment reflects the value of an already-developed terminal; the reporting records those concerns but does not establish the alleged future losses.[2][3]

Our answer is conditional. Retained ownership alone is insufficient to prove a good deal, but foreign operation alone is insufficient to prove a bad one. Some information may require commercial redaction; that does not prevent proposing publication of measurable targets, audited receipts and a clear account of public obligations. This is our disclosure recommendation, not a claim that a particular law requires full release.
