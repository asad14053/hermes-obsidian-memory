## TOPIC

**AI ব্যর্থ, নাকি মাপার পদ্ধতিতে সমস্যা?**

A Bangla explainer about evaluating AI through a concrete scientific case: predicting how gene activity changes after a genetic intervention. The October 1 Nature Biotechnology paper reports that some models reveal useful perturbation-specific signal under better-calibrated metrics, while a strong simple baseline remains hard to beat in some settings.[1]

Scope: research benchmarks of gene-expression prediction. Not all AI, a clinical diagnostic test, a demonstration of a complete virtual human cell, or evidence of a treatment breakthrough.

## CORE THESIS

Before interpreting an AI score, ask what the score rewards and whether it can separate an informative prediction from a deliberately uninformative one. The paper tests this with positive and negative controls rather than simply choosing the metric with the most flattering model ranking.[1]

Editorial conclusion: a disappointing score may indicate a weak model, an insensitive measurement, or both. A favorable score still needs a meaningful baseline and a test connected to the intended use. “Better benchmark result” and “useful treatment discovery” are not interchangeable.

## STORY STRUCTURE

1. **Freeze the prediction, change the score.** Open with the apparent contradiction. Explain that a scoring rule is a question, not a neutral stamp of intelligence.
2. **Make the biology visible.** Show gene-activity bars before and after a genetic intervention. Explain the mean baseline: it gives every test perturbation the same average profile learned from training perturbations.[1]
3. **Test the test.** Show a negative control alongside an informative positive control. Explain why unchanged genes can dominate an error average and dilute the contribution of the changed genes.[1]
4. **Give both studies a fair hearing.** Calibrated metrics can expose model signal, but an additive baseline remains hard to beat on Norman19; different datasets, models and training splits also matter.[1][2]
5. **End on what still has to be proved.** The newer paper does not address unseen cell types, donors or conditions.[1] Give viewers a checklist: prediction target, control, comparator, held-out test and intended use.

## COUNTERARGUMENT / ALTERNATIVE VIEW

The strongest objection is that one can make a preferred model look better by choosing favorable weights or highlighting only responsive genes. A benchmark also needs to punish false changes and show useful performance on a task that matters. This is an editorial methodological objection, not an allegation against the authors.

The paper's answer is to assess whether metrics separate positive from negative controls and to retain complementary metric families.[1] It explicitly warns against treating a single well-calibrated metric as definitive and retains direct error as a realism check.[1] That makes “throw out MSE and declare victory” an inaccurate account of its recommendations.

The older paper's negative findings remain evidence for its tested setup.[2] The newer study itself retains a difficult-to-beat additive baseline and does not test unseen contexts.[1] Our verdict: calibration improves the question we ask; it does not settle every claim about biological generalization.
