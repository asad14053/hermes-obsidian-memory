## TOPIC

**AI-এর নতুন bottleneck: মানুষের অনুমতি**

Angle: follow one proposed AI data center through six gates, then test OpenAI's new promises against the objections voters raised.

## CORE THESIS

AI companies have spent years treating chips, electricity and capital as the main limits on growth. The November 3 results expose another limit: communities can delay, restrict or reject the physical infrastructure behind AI.[2][3]

OpenAI's response is unusually concrete. For future campuses it owns and operates, the company promises to pay its electricity and infrastructure costs, protect other customers if a project shrinks or fails, publish site-level energy and water use, commission noise studies and stop using broad NDAs with public officials.[1]

But the compact is voluntary and narrow. OpenAI itself says broader rules are needed, while the wording covers campuses it owns and operates rather than every facility built for its capacity.[1]

The video should ask a practical question: does the compact solve the public problem, or does it only make a better offer?

## WHY NOW

On November 3, voters considered 41 local data-center measures in ten states. Reuters reported that all but one went against data centers.[2]

The direct Ballotpedia inventory shows why the result needs careful wording: the measures were not identical. Its 2026 list includes full bans, partial bans, moratoriums and advisory questions.[3]

On November 5, OpenAI published a National Community Compact and policy blueprint for future campuses it owns and operates.[1]

On November 6, Reuters reported that incoming congressional Democrats plan bills on data-center electricity costs, independent AI testing and liability. Those proposals are plans, not enacted law, and Reuters says they face steep political odds.[4]

The best publish window is November 7-9. After that, the topic still works, but the opening should shift from election news to the six-gate infrastructure framework.

## KEY EVIDENCE

### E1: The ballot result is large, but the measures differ

Reuters reported that all but one of 41 measures voted on that week went against data centers.[2]

Ballotpedia's direct inventory covers at least 50 data-center measures across 13 states during 2026, not one national referendum. Its November analysis includes full bans, partial bans and moratoriums, while the underlying table also includes advisory questions.[3]

Evidence boundary: the result shows strong local resistance to many current projects and terms. It does not prove that 40 communities oppose every data center or every form of AI infrastructure.

### E2: OpenAI promises to pay its own energy and infrastructure costs

OpenAI says every future campus it owns and operates will have a local compact, and that it will pay the full cost of electricity plus infrastructure needed to serve the site.[1]

The company also promises financial assurances before major spending so other customers are protected if a project is delayed, reduced or canceled.[1]

Evidence boundary: these are OpenAI's stated commitments. No utility tariff, commission order or site-level audit was reviewed here to prove how the promise will work in each location.

### E3: The compact includes disclosure and NDA limits

OpenAI promises site-level water and electricity reporting, construction plans, independent noise studies and no NDAs with public officials beyond narrow protections for security or commercially sensitive information.[1]

Evidence boundary: the announcement describes future practice. It does not yet provide a completed annual report or an independent compliance audit.

### E4: The ownership wording matters

The compact repeatedly applies to future campuses OpenAI owns and operates.[1]

The final script must map each example site's owner, operator, developer, utility customer and capacity buyer. A campus dedicated to OpenAI is not automatically an OpenAI-owned-and-operated campus.

### E5: OpenAI says voluntary commitments are not enough

OpenAI calls company commitments a starting point and asks policymakers to create rules for large electricity users, public reporting, NDAs and tax incentives.[1]

That makes the strongest test straightforward: identify which promises are contractual, regulatory, statutory or merely public commitments.

### E6: Congress may act, but nothing in this brief is enacted law

Reuters reports planned bills on electricity costs, independent model testing and AI liability.[4]

The plans remain politically uncertain. The script must not say Congress has passed a data-center cost law or a federal AI testing mandate.[4]

## COUNTERARGUMENT / ALTERNATIVE VIEW

The election results may reflect specific zoning terms, taxes, water concerns or individual projects rather than broad opposition to AI. Some questions were advisory, and one result favored the industry.[2][3]

Data centers can bring construction work, tax revenue and computing capacity. OpenAI argues that more capacity can lower the cost of AI and support local jobs and businesses.[1] Those benefits should be tested with project-specific numbers rather than dismissed or repeated as promises.

A voluntary compact can still matter. It can create a public baseline, shape permit conditions and make later failures easier to identify. The limitation is that a company statement is not the same as a tariff, permit, contract or law.

The fair conclusion is not "stop every data center." It is that the industry now has to prove who benefits, who pays and who can enforce the answer.
