## TOPIC

**রোবট কাজ করতে পারা আর মানুষকে সরিয়ে লাভ হওয়া এক জিনিস নয়।**

A Bangla explainer about task capability, deployment cost, and actual job change. The case study estimates that current robots could perform tasks representing 34% of US working time in some circumstances, but would be cost-competitive for only 0.3% of all working time under its assumptions.[11]

Scope: a US-focused, model-assisted research estimate, not a census of installed robots, a global job-loss forecast, or a Bangladesh employment estimate. “Robots” includes specialized autonomous machines, not just humanoids.[11]

## CORE THESIS

The useful question is not just whether a robot can complete a demonstration. Ask whether it can do the relevant tasks in the actual workplace, at comparable output and reliability, for less total cost. The report explicitly distinguishes capability from cost and includes human supervision and maintenance in deployment estimates.[11]

Editorial argument: a capability headline leaves out most of the employer's decision. However, an economy-wide estimate of low cost competitiveness is not an assurance that any individual occupation is protected. Keep task exposure, economic feasibility, adoption, and job displacement separate throughout.

## STORY STRUCTURE

1. **One box is not a whole job.** Begin with a deliberately limited task demonstration. Establish the question: capability, cost, or job loss?
2. **Put the denominators on screen.** The report's 74% concerns physical work; 34% concerns all work time; 0.3% is its cost-competitive share of all work time.[11] Do not put 74% and 0.3% on the same unlabeled bar.
3. **Build the cost sheet.** Use the report's packer example to show purchase, installation, financing/service life, maintenance, energy, and human supervision.[11] Move from “price per robot” to “cost for the same yearly output.”
4. **Audit the estimate.** Explain Claude's role, then reveal the appendix sensitivity: removing ratings based on related rather than exact robot tasks lowers exposed physical work from roughly three-quarters to half.[18] This challenges the apparent precision without dismissing the whole study.
5. **Give a bounded verdict.** Costs and capability can change, and some occupations may face pressure much sooner than the aggregate suggests.[11] End with a decision checklist, not “all jobs are doomed” or “nothing to worry about.”

### Internal checks

- Denominator rule: use **34% versus 0.3% of total US working time** for the main comparison. Introduce 74% only after explaining physical versus total work.
- The report rounds category shares; do not reconstruct exact totals from rounded figures or show unnecessary decimal precision.
- Illustrative packer calculation, executed in Python: $49,000 × 0.97 = $47,530 of task-matched compensation; $47,530 − $45,000 = $2,530, consistent with the paper's rounded “about $2,500.”[11] Inputs are rounded estimates; do not treat $2,530 as an independently measured saving.
- The paper's capability sensitivity and cost omissions are visible in its appendix; retain them rather than reducing the story to two headline percentages.[18]
- An association between historical exposure and later employment changes is not sufficient to attribute every observed job decline to robots. We omit the historical causal claim from the opening.

## COUNTERARGUMENT / ALTERNATIVE VIEW

The strongest counterargument is that today's cost model may become obsolete: better robots, cheaper production, lower supervision needs and redesigned jobs could change the calculation. The authors explicitly allow that more capable robots or manufacturing changes could accelerate cost declines; their stylized scenarios do not predict all such changes.[11][18]

The estimate can also be challenged on its present assumptions. Counting related-task robots expands the capability headline materially, while some human employment costs are omitted.[18] These limitations point in different directions; it is not defensible to declare a precise corrected number without rerunning the model.

Our answer: use the report to explain the questions an automation claim must answer, not to promise a timetable. An employer can automate a particular task even when the economy-wide share is small. This is an interpretation, not individualized career or financial advice.

Draft rewrite: “রোবট আপনার চাকরি নেবে? নতুন গবেষণায় দেখা যাচ্ছে, অধিকাংশ কাজেই মানুষ সস্তা।”

What makes the draft weak:

- It turns a conditional estimate into an observed fact.
- “অধিকাংশ কাজ” hides the US scope and task-time denominator.
- It sounds like reassurance before explaining individual exposure.

Final rewrite: “রোবট আপনার কাজটা করতে পারে। কিন্তু মানুষকে সরিয়ে সেটা করালে খরচ কমবে? নতুন গবেষণায় এই দুই প্রশ্নের উত্তর এক নয়।”[11]
